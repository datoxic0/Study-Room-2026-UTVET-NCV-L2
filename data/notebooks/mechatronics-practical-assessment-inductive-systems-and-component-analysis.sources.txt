TASK ONE: Functional Component Analysis
While the physical assessment requires you to identify these items in a workshop,
understanding their roles within a broader mechatronics or electrotechnology ecosystem is
crucial. Here is an advanced categorization of the 15 items listed on your assessment sheet:
1. Power, Actuation & Pneumatics
 Power Supply Unit (PSU): Converts standard AC mains power into regulated DC
voltage, providing the stable electrical energy required for low-voltage control circuits
and DC actuators.
 12V DC Motor: The primary electromechanical actuator. It serves as the inductive
load that translates electrical current into rotary mechanical motion.
 Air Compressor & Manifold: These represent the pneumatic power counterparts to
electrical systems. The compressor generates pressurized air, and the manifold acts
as a centralized distribution hub to route this air to various pneumatic cylinders or
valves.
2. Control & Switching
 Relay: An electrically operated switch. It allows a low-power control signal (like one
from a microcontroller) to safely switch a high-power load (like the 12V DC motor) by
utilizing an electromagnet to mechanically close or open contacts.
 Push Button (Red/Green): Momentary mechanical switches. Green typically
features Normally Open (NO) contacts for initiating a process ("Start"), while Red
features Normally Closed (NC) contacts for halting a process ("Stop" or "E-Stop").
 Terminal Block: A modular, insulated block that secures two or more wires together,
providing a safe, organized, and easily modifiable point for circuit junctions.
3. Diagnostics, Indication & Tooling
 Multimeter: An essential diagnostic instrument used to measure voltage, current,
and resistance. It is critical for verifying PSU output, testing relay coil continuity, and
troubleshooting circuit faults.
 Indication Lamp (Yellow/Green): Provides immediate visual feedback on system
status (e.g., Green for "System Energized/Running", Yellow for "Standby" or
"Warning").
 Flat Screwdriver & Tool Box: The mechanical interface tools required for securing
wire terminals, mounting components, and housing equipment.
4. Safety & Maintenance
 First Aid Box: Mandatory safety equipment for immediate response to workshop

 Spray Paint: Often used in industrial settings for color-coding components, marking

3. Energy Conversion Analysis (The Physics)
The process of converting electrical energy into rotary mechanical energy relies heavily on
electromagnetic induction and the Lorentz force law.
When the switch is closed, direct current ($I$) flows through the copper windings (the
armature) located inside the motor's housing. These windings are situated within a
permanent magnetic field ($B$) provided by the motor's stator.
According to the Lorentz force principle, a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic
field experiences a mechanical force ($F$). For a straight conductor of length $L$, the force
is defined by the cross product:
$$F = I L \times B$$
Because the windings are arranged in loops on a central rotor, this force acts in opposite
directions on either side of the loop, generating a torque ($\tau$) that causes the motor shaft
to rotate.
Simultaneously, as the armature rotates through the magnetic field, it acts as a generator,
inducing a voltage in the opposite direction of the supplied voltage. This is known as Back
Electromotive Force (Back EMF). The Back EMF ($E_b$) is proportional to the angular
velocity ($\omega$) of the motor:
$$E_b = k \omega$$
(Where $k$ is the motor constant). This Back EMF is crucial as it self-regulates the current
drawn by the motor once it reaches its operating speed, preventing the windings from
overheating.
4. Application and Understanding
Understanding this simple inductive system is the foundational building block for advanced
mechatronics. In practical, real-world applications, this exact principle is used in:
 Actuators in Robotics: Driving the joints and wheels of automated guided vehicles
(AGVs).
 Industrial Automation: Powering conveyor belts and automated sorting
mechanisms.
 System Cooling: Driving the rotary fans in server racks and computer hardware.
In modern systems, rather than a simple push-button, these motors are controlled via
software logic, using microcontrollers to send Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) signals to
motor driver ICs, allowing for precise control over the motor's RPM and direction.
