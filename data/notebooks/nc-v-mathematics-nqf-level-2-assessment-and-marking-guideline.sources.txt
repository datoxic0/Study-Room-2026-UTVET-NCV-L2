Part 2: Answer Key
1. Rational vs. Irrational Decimals: A rational number has a "tail" that either terminates (stops) or recurs (repeats a pattern), whereas an irrational number has a non-terminating and non-recurring decimal tail that continues forever without repeating. This makes rational numbers representable as clean fractions, while irrational numbers like \pi or \sqrt{30} cannot be.
2. The Importance of "Middle Work": Markers use a hierarchy of Method (M), Accuracy (A), and Continued Accuracy (CA) marks, meaning credit is awarded for the "pathway" to the value. Showing calculations allows a student to receive CA marks if a minor error occurs early on but the subsequent logic remains correct.
3. The Rule of 1: To rationalize a monomial denominator, you multiply the fraction by a "special version of 1," which is the denominator's surd divided by itself (e.g., \frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{6}}). This changes the appearance of the expression to remove the radical from the bottom without changing the expression's actual value.
4. Growth Patterns: Simple interest follows a linear growth pattern where the investment increases by a fixed amount every year based only on the original principal. Compound interest follows an exponential growth pattern because interest is earned on both the principal and the previously accumulated interest, causing the value to accelerate over time.
5. Eliminating Recurring Tails: To convert a recurring decimal, you assign it a variable x, then multiply by powers of 10 (like 10, 100, or 1000) to shift the decimal so that the repeating digits align perfectly. By subtracting the smaller equation from the larger one, the infinite repeating "tail" is eliminated, leaving a solvable linear equation.
6. Time Conversion in Finance: The variable n in interest formulas must always be expressed in years to align with the "per annum" interest rate. Failing to convert 48 months into 4 years (48 \div 12) will lead to an incorrect calculation and typically results in zero marks for the question.
7. The Conjugate: A conjugate consists of the same two terms as a binomial denominator but with the opposite sign (e.g., 1 + \sqrt{2} becomes 1 - \sqrt{2}). It is used to rationalize binomial denominators by creating a "Difference of Two Squares," which effectively squares the surds and removes the radicals.
8. Simple vs. Compound Performance: While compound interest is more powerful over long periods, a simple interest model with a higher interest rate (e.g., 7%) can yield a higher final maturity value than a compound model with a lower rate (e.g., 6.4%) over a short duration like three years.
9. Deriving the Interest Rate (i): The interest rate i is the decimal version of the percentage rate r, calculated using the formula i = \frac{r}{100}. This conversion must be completed before the value is plugged into the growth formulas A = P(1+in) or A = P(1+i)^n.
10. Rational Form Requirements: For a number to be rational, it must be expressible as \frac{a}{b} where both a and b are elements of Z (integers) and the denominator b is not equal to zero.

Part 4: Glossary of Key Terms
Term Definition
Compound Interest A financial model where interest is calculated on the initial principal and also on the accumulated interest of previous periods, resulting in exponential growth.
Conjugate A binomial formed by taking the same two terms of another binomial and changing the sign between them; used to rationalize denominators.
Continued Accuracy (CA) A marking principle where a student receives credit for correctly following a mathematical procedure even if they are using an incorrect value carried over from a previous step.
Irrational Number A number that cannot be expressed as a fraction \frac{a}{b} and has a non-terminating, non-recurring decimal expansion.
Principal (P) The original sum of money invested or borrowed before interest is applied.
Rational Number Any number that can be expressed as the quotient \frac{a}{b} of two integers, where the denominator b is not zero.
Rationalize The process of eliminating radicals (surds) from the denominator of an algebraic fraction to reach its simplest form.
Recurring Decimal A decimal fraction in which a figure or group of figures is repeated indefinitely.
Simple Interest A financial model where interest is calculated only on the original amount of money invested or borrowed, resulting in linear growth.
Surd An irrational number that is the root of a positive integer (e.g., \sqrt{2}) and cannot be simplified to a whole number or a terminating/recurring decimal.
Terminating Decimal A decimal that has a finite number of digits after the decimal comma.

Question: Convert $121,515151...$ to the form $\frac{a}{b}$ where $a, b \in Z$ and $b \neq 0$. Write the final solution in its
simplest form.
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Let $x = 121.515151...$ (Equation 1)
2. Since two decimal places are repeating, multiply both sides by 100 to shift the decimal point:
$100x = 12151.515151...$ (Equation 2)
3. Subtract Equation 1 from Equation 2 to eliminate the recurring decimal part:
$$100x - x = 12151.515151... - 121.515151...$$
$$99x = 12030$$
4. Solve for $x$:
$$x = \frac{12030}{99}$$
5. Simplify the fraction by finding the greatest common divisor. Both numbers are divisible by 3:
$$x = \frac{12030 \div 3}{99 \div 3} = \frac{4010}{33}$$

QUESTION 1.3: Simplifying Surds
1.3.1 $\frac{\sqrt{54} + 5\sqrt{6} - 3\sqrt{24}}{\sqrt{150}}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Break down each surd into its prime factors to find perfect squares:
o $\sqrt{54} = \sqrt{9 \times 6} = 3\sqrt{6}$

o $3\sqrt{24} = 3\sqrt{4 \times 6} = 3 \times 2\sqrt{6} = 6\sqrt{6}$

2. Substitute the simplified surds back into the expression:
$$\frac{3\sqrt{6} + 5\sqrt{6} - 6\sqrt{6}}{5\sqrt{6}}$$
3. Combine the like terms in the numerator:
$$\frac{(3 + 5 - 6)\sqrt{6}}{5\sqrt{6}} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{5\sqrt{6}}$$
4. Cancel out the common $\sqrt{6}$ term:
Final Answer: $\frac{2}{5}$
1.3.2 $\frac{\sqrt{8}}{1 - \sqrt{5}}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Simplify the numerator: $\sqrt{8} = \sqrt{4 \times 2} = 2\sqrt{2}$.
2. Rationalize the denominator by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the conjugate, which is $(1 +
\sqrt{5})$:
$$\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{(1 - \sqrt{5})} \times \frac{(1 + \sqrt{5})}{(1 + \sqrt{5})}$$
3. Expand the denominator using the difference of squares formula $(a-b)(a+b) = a^2 - b^2$:
$$1^2 - (\sqrt{5})^2 = 1 - 5 = -4$$
4. Expand the numerator:
$$2\sqrt{2}(1 + \sqrt{5}) = 2\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{10}$$
5. Combine and simplify:
$$\frac{2\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{10}}{-4}$$
Factor out the 2 in the numerator:
$$\frac{2(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{10})}{-4} = \frac{\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{10}}{-2}$$
Final Answer: $-\frac{\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{10}}{2}$

1.4.2 $\frac{4^{x} + 4^{x+2}}{4^{x}}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Use the law of exponents in reverse to split the second term in the numerator: $4^{x+2} = 4^{x} \cdot 4^{2}$.

$$\frac{4^{x}(1 + 4^{2})}{4^{x}}$$
3. Cancel out $4^{x}$ from the numerator and denominator:
$$1 + 4^{2} = 1 + 16$$

1.4.3 $\frac{(a^{4})^{n+1} \times (a^{2}b)^{-3n}}{(ab)^{-2n} \times b^{-n}}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Apply the power of a power law ($(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$) and distribute exponents into the brackets:
o Numerator: $a^{4(n+1)} \times a^{2(-3n)}b^{-3n} = a^{4n+4} \times a^{-6n}b^{-3n}$
o Denominator: $a^{-2n}b^{-2n} \times b^{-n}$

o Numerator: $a^{4n+4-6n}b^{-3n} = a^{-2n+4}b^{-3n}$
o Denominator: $a^{-2n}b^{-2n-n} = a^{-2n}b^{-3n}$
3. Divide using the quotient law ($a^{m} \div a^{n} = a^{m-n}$):
$$\frac{a^{-2n+4}b^{-3n}}{a^{-2n}b^{-3n}} = a^{-2n+4 - (-2n)} \times b^{-3n - (-3n)}$$
$$= a^{-2n+4+2n} \times b^{-3n+3n} = a^{4} \times b^{0}$$

Final Answer: $a^{4}$

Converting Terminating Decimals

The "Algebraic Magic" for Recurring Decimals
To convert recurring decimals, we use variable assignment to "kill" the infinite tail.
* Example 1 (0,6\dot{8}):
1. Let x = 0,6888...
2. Multiply by 10 to shift the comma to the start of the recurrence: 10x = 6,8888...
3. Multiply by 100 to move the first repeating digit: 100x = 68,8888...
4. Subtract: 100x - 10x = 68,888... - 6,888... \implies 90x = 62.
5. Simplify: x = \frac{62}{90} = \frac{31}{45}.

1. Let x = 2,586586...
2. 1000x = 2586,586586...
3. Subtract: 1000x - x = 2586,586... - 2,586... \implies 999x = 2584.
4. Final form: x = \frac{2584}{999}.
This precision is critical; using the wrong multiplier will fail to align the tails, resulting in an unsolvable expression.

NQF Level 2 Foundation: Mastering Rational and Irrational Numbers
1. Introduction to the Number System
In the vocational landscape—whether you are calculating electrical tolerances, measuring materials on a construction site, or determining compound interest for a business loan—the number system is the mathematical bedrock upon which your success is built. Understanding the nature of numbers is the "secret key" to mastering more complex operations like exponents and surds. If you cannot identify the tools you are working with, you cannot choose the right method to solve the problem.
Learning Objective By the end of this primer, you will be able to distinguish between rational and irrational numbers, convert terminating and recurring decimals into their fractional forms, and rationalize denominators to ensure your final expressions meet NQF Level 2 examination standards.
Before we can apply these numbers to technical drawings or financial balance sheets, we must understand the fundamental divide that categorizes every number you will encounter.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The "So What?": Identifying Numbers at a Glance
To succeed in a timed exam, you need to identify these at a glance by looking at their decimal "tails":
• Rational Numbers: These either terminate (stop completely, like 1,14) or recur (have a repeating pattern, like 0,6888...).
• Irrational Numbers: These are non-terminating and non-recurring. They continue forever without any repeating pattern, much like the measurements found in complex circular geometry.

4. The Algebraic Magic: Converting Recurring Decimals
When a number has a repeating "tail," we use algebra to isolate and eliminate the infinite part.
Pro-Tip: Choosing your Multiplier We use multipliers like 10x, 100x, or 1000x to shift the decimal comma. Crucially, you must choose a multiplier that ensures the repeating digits start immediately after the comma in both equations so they align perfectly for subtraction.

5. Working with Irrationals: The Role of Surds and Rationalization
In vocational mathematics, irrational numbers often appear as surds (roots that aren't whole numbers). It is a standard convention to "Rationalize the Denominator," which means moving the irrational root from the bottom of a fraction to the top to make the expression easier to work with.
