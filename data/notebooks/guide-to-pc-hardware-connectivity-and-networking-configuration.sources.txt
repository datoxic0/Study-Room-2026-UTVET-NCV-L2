Unit 4.7: Environment conditions
for setting up a PC
When you set up a computer, make sure that the area where you want to put it is computer-friendly. As mentioned in the previous section on cleaning, liquids can damage electronics, so make sure that the room housing the computer is dry and not damp.
The environment should also not be excessively dirty, because airborne particles such as filings or sawdust can clog fans and jam the moving components of a computer.
Place the computer on a stable, solid surface that has sufficient surrounding space to accomodate all its auxilliaries.
Make sure that none of the vents are blocked or covered, since this can cause overheating. If something flammable is blocking a vent, this object might even catch fire!
Position the computer as close as possible to a suitable power supply. Although extension cables can be used to connect a computer to a power outlet, they often get in the way and may even cause people to trip. Another environmental factor that may damage your hard drive is static electricity. During dry weather conditions, high electric potentials can be generated by static electricity. A person loaded with static electricity who touches any of the computer's components may cause one or more of the integrated circuits to blow. You can prevent this happening by using anti-static mats, chairs or bracelets to prevent the static from building up.
Another external influence which may severely damage your computer is a surge in electric power due to variations in the power grid. Since these surges can hardly be prevented, the only effective way to protect computers is to use a surge protection device in the power supply of each computer.

In computer networking, we use the word 'topology' to layout of connected devices. In a LAN, these LAN, these computers are connected in a star, bus or ring configuration, as shown in Fig. 4.47.
Bus Topology
Star Topology
Ring Topology
Fig. 4.47: Different LAN topologies
Let's look at each of these different arrangements.
Star topology
A star network is one of the most common network setups, in which each of the devices and computers on a network connect to a central hub, switch or Router. The major disadvantage of this type of network topology is that if the central hub fails, all computers connected to that hub will be disconnected. However, a failure in any star network cable will only take down one computer's network access and not the entire LAN.
Bus topology
A bus topology, such as 10Base-2 or 10Base-5 Ethernet, is a type of network setup in which each of the computers and network devices is connected to a single cable or 'backbone'. When one device wants to communicate with another device on the network, it broadcasts a message onto the wire that all other devices 'see'. However, only the intended recipient actually accepts and processes the message. Bus networks work best with a limited number of devices, since performance problems will likely result if more than a few dozen computers are connected to the backbone.
Words & Terms
topology: the physical configuration of a network that determines how the network's computers, or nodes, are arranged and connected to each other
Ring topology
In a ring topology, each device has only its two neighbours (one on either side) for communication purposes and all the messages travel through a ring in the same direction. In a ring topology, a packet is sent around the ring until it reaches its final destination.
A disadvantage of a ring network is that a failure in any cable or device breaks the loop and can take down the entire network. Therefore, this topology is seldom used. FDDI or SONET are examples of ring topologies.
The shape (topology) of a network does not necessarily correspond to the physical layout of the devices. For example, although the computers in a home LAN can be placed in a circle in the family room,
this does not mean that it is a ring topology network.

Twisted pair cables
As its name suggests, a twisted pair cable consists of two wires that are insulated separately and twisted around each other. It is the cheapest option for a LAN network.
Coaxial cables
Fig. 4.48: Twisted pair cables
This type of cable consists of a central wire that is surrounded by an insulation tube. Coaxial cables usually consist of many layers - the centre wire will be surrounded by insulation, which itself is surrounded by braided wire (a shielding layer), which is again surrounded by insulation. The shielding layer prevents outside electrical interference.
This type of cable can carry a lot more data than a twisted pair cable and is less susceptible to interference.
Fig. 4.49: Coaxial cable
Fibre optic cables
Fibre optic cables are made up of bundles of glass or plastic threads. They work by transmitting light waves, which are then converted into an electrical signal at the other end of the cable.
Fibre optical cables transmit data at higher rates than the previously mentioned cables; they also have an extremely high bandwidth.
Wireless system
A wireless network, as its name implies, has no wires connecting the computers belonging to a network. Instead, these computers communicate by means of radio or microwaves that move invisibly through the air.

Ultimate Study Guide: PC Hardware Identification, Connectivity, and Configuration
Introduction: The Strategic Role of Hardware Architecture
In the professional IT landscape, hardware mastery is not merely a technical skill but a foundational competency that drives organizational efficiency. In 1977, Ken Olson, the founder of Digital Equipment Corporation, famously remarked that there was "no reason anyone would want a computer in their home." This perspective shifted radically within just three decades; today, personal computers outnumber people in many regions. For the modern IT professional, understanding the hardware lifecycle—from interpreting manufacturer specifications to executing safe physical deployments—is critical for managing the complex links that allow these machines to communicate and function.
As defined in the professional curriculum, hardware competency requires the following core competencies:
Hardware Synthesis: Identifying components and explaining connectivity procedures alongside essential safety precautions.
System Integration: Aligning relevant software functions with specific computer parameters and hardware specifications.
Interface Expertise: Distinguishing between cable types (Serial, Parallel, USB) and extension cards (Video, Sound, Networking).
Infrastructure Design: Explaining the principles of computer networks, including topologies (Star, Bus, Ring) and transmission media.
Security & Risk Management: Implementing antivirus procedures, environmental controls, and cryptographic protocols to protect data integrity.
Quality Assurance: Utilizing professional checklists to verify physical connections and environmental suitability.
The starting point for mastering these competencies is the motherboard, the central circuit board where all system communication originates and concludes.

These specifications serve as the blueprint for system assembly, dictating the compatibility of every other component. Understanding these parameters ensures an IT professional can prevent performance bottlenecks and plan viable upgrade paths.

Memory (RAM) and Processor (CPU) Ecosystems
The CPU (the brain) and RAM (the active data storage) operate in a symbiotic relationship regulated by the motherboard chipset. The chipset acts as the communication manager, typically divided into two specific logical components:
Northbridge: Handles high-speed communication between the CPU, RAM, and graphics (AGP) slots.
Southbridge: Manages slower I/O functions, including peripheral ports and storage controllers.
RAM Categories and Data Transfer Characteristics
DDR (Double Data Rate): Transfers data on both the rising and falling edges of the clock signal, effectively doubling the transmission rate.
SDRAM (Synchronous Dynamic RAM): Synchronizes with the CPU bus to allow data transfers up to 133 MHz.
RDRAM (Rambus Dynamic RAM): A proprietary high-speed bandwidth design capable of 1.6 GB/s.
DRAM (Dynamic RAM): The standard memory type where each bit is stored in a separate capacitor.
EDO (Extended Data Out): Capable of transferring multiple blocks of data simultaneously rather than one at a time, improving efficiency over standard DRAM.
Chipset and CPU Compatibility
Processor selection (e.g., AMD Athlon 64 FX) is dictated by the motherboard's socket and chipset. A chipset like the VIA K8T800Pro is engineered to interface with specific architectures. Choosing an incompatible combination prevents system operation and limits the professional's ability to upgrade the system's processing power in the future.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Physical Connectivity: Ports, Cables, and Interface Standards
Standardized connectivity protocols ensure hardware interoperability. Because of worldwide standards, components from different manufacturers can communicate using established "languages" (protocols) through physical motherboard interfaces.
Master Identification Guide for Cables and Connectors
Video Interfaces
VGA (Video Graphics Array): An analogue standard. The cable contains 15 wires and a connector with 15 pins organized in three rows of five.
DVI (Digital Visual Interface): A digital standard for flat panels and projectors. The cable contains 25 wires, with a connector featuring one single pin plus three rows of eight pins.
S-Video (Separated Video): An analogue signal that carries video data separately. Commonly uses 3 RCA plugs for TV connections and is a standard fitting for gaming consoles like the XBOX, Wii, and Playstation.
Peripheral Ports
Serial: Transfers data one bit at a time. Uses D-sub-miniature connectors with 9 or 25 pins.
Parallel (LPT): Sends several signals simultaneously. The cable is thick and stiff, containing 25 wires with large connectors. Primarily used for legacy printers.
USB (Universal Serial Bus): A versatile replacement for serial/parallel ports, using only 4 wires plus a shielding wire.
Data Standards (USB)
Type A: The standard connector found on the computer/host side.
Type B: The most popular connector for the device side (e.g., printers and scanners).
Diagnostic "So What?" of USB Technology
USB is the industry standard due to its versatility and ability to supply power.
