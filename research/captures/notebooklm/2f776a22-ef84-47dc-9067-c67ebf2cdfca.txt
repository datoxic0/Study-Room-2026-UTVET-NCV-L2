To expand your understanding of how this works in practice:
Single Path for Current: In a series circuit, electrical components are connected end-to-end, creating one continuous loop. Because there is only one path for the electricity to take, the current must travel through every single resistor sequentially.
Cumulative Opposition: Since the current is forced to pass through each component, every resistor adds its own opposition to the flow of electrons. Because there are no alternate routes or branches for the current to bypass a resistor, the total opposition (total resistance) is simply the sum of all the individual resistors combined.
Constant Current, Divided Voltage: Due to this single sequential path, the current (I) remains the exact same through every component in a series circuit. However, the total voltage provided by the power source is divided among the resistors based on their individual resistance values, which is known as a "voltage drop."
In short, whenever you see components connected in a single line (series), you just add their values together to find the total resistance because the current has to push through every single one of them.

A basic transformer consists of two separate coils of wire—the primary (input) coil and the secondary (output) coil—wrapped around a magnetic core. It works through electromagnetic induction: an alternating current (AC) in the primary coil creates a fluctuating magnetic field, which in turn induces a voltage in the secondary coil.
